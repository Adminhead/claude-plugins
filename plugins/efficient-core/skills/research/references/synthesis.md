# Synthesis, conflict, and confidence

Synthesis answers the decision question; it does not merely list sources. Keep evidence, interpretation, and recommendation distinguishable so a reader can see what follows from what.

## Classify statements

Use clear wording or labels for these statement types:

- **Sourced fact:** a source directly states, documents, or measures it. Cite it.
- **Observation:** a recorded item from supplied material, such as an interview, survey response, ticket, field note, or document. Preserve its context and provenance.
- **Inference:** your conclusion from facts or observations. State the reasoning and do not cite it as though a source made the conclusion.
- **Recommendation:** a proposed action or choice based on stated criteria, evidence, trade-offs, and uncertainty.
- **Unknown or assumption:** information that is absent, inaccessible, disputed, or temporarily assumed. State what would update it when material.

Do not make causal, universal, current, or comparative claims stronger than the evidence supports.

## Build the answer around the decision

A concise research answer usually contains:

1. **Direct answer:** the conclusion, recommendation, or bounded explanation.
2. **Key support:** the few facts or observations that most affect it, with citations.
3. **Reasoning and trade-offs:** how the support meets or misses the stated criteria; label inferences.
4. **Confidence and limits:** source quality, applicability, conflict, assumptions, and as-of date when relevant.
5. **Next decision-relevant gap:** only when it would materially change the answer.

Adapt this structure to the requested deliverable. Default to a brief answer; a research note, detailed comparison, or formal findings file is appropriate only when requested or required by local convention.

## Resolve or present disagreement

When sources differ, do not select the most convenient result. First check whether the apparent conflict comes from different definitions, populations, methods, jurisdictions, versions, dates, incentives, or outcomes measured. Then:

- prefer the source that is more direct, current, applicable, and transparent about method for the claim at hand;
- report a qualified conclusion if the evidence resolves the difference;
- present the competing evidence and the decision consequence when it does not; and
- state what evidence would discriminate between alternatives if that is known.

Avoid false precision. Do not average incompatible measurements or treat a numerical majority of sources as proof.

## Express confidence proportionately

Confidence is a communication aid, not a calculation unless the task supplies a valid method. Ground it in the strength, directness, recency, applicability, independence, and agreement of the evidence.

- **Higher confidence:** direct and applicable evidence is current enough, independently corroborated where material, and has no unresolved contradiction that changes the conclusion.
- **Qualified confidence:** evidence supports a conclusion but has an important limitation, assumption, or scope restriction.
- **Low confidence / unresolved:** access gaps, indirect evidence, disagreement, or poor applicability prevents a reliable conclusion.

Name the limiting factor instead of using a confidence label alone.

## Synthesize existing qualitative or user research

When the inputs are existing interviews, surveys, usability notes, support tickets, or similar qualitative material, treat them as supplied evidence rather than conducting new external research.

1. Define the research question, population, time range, and decision use.
2. Extract observations with enough context to preserve who or what they represent; distinguish participant statements from researcher interpretation.
3. Group observations into themes or patterns, checking for disconfirming cases and variation across relevant segments.
4. Consider frequency, impact, and confidence separately. Frequent reports are not automatically severe; a small number of high-impact observations can matter.
5. Surface contradictions, sampling limitations, missing segments, and uncertainty.
6. Make specific recommendations only where the observations and stated decision criteria support them.

Do not generalize a theme beyond the sample or claim statistical prevalence from qualitative counts alone. Cite or identify the supplied evidence in a privacy-preserving form consistent with the task’s constraints.
